L01 · Exposure triangledeveloping

What was learned.

Exposure is controlled by three linked choices. Aperture changes how much light passes through the lens and influences depth of field. Shutter speed controls how long light reaches the sensor and whether movement is frozen or blurred. ISO changes amplification: raising it can make a dim scene usable but usually adds noise. A practical order is to protect the creative constraint first—motion or depth of field—then raise ISO only as much as needed.

Practice.

For a moving person indoors, choose between A: 1/30 s, f/2.8, ISO 800 and B: 1/125 s, f/2.8, ISO 3200. Explain the trade-off.

Learner submission.

I chose A because ISO 800 should look cleaner, although the person may blur.

Feedback.

The noise trade-off was identified correctly, but the task is a moving person. B is safer because 1/125 s reduces motion blur; some extra noise is preferable to an unusably blurred subject.

L02 · Composition and visual attentiondemonstrated

What was learned.

Composition directs attention. Start by naming the subject, then inspect the frame edges and background for competing elements. The rule of thirds is a useful prompt, not a law. Leading lines work only when they lead toward the subject. Moving a step left or lowering the camera often changes a frame more than changing settings.

Practice.

Make three versions of one scene: centered, off-center, and with a leading line. Choose one and explain why.

Learner submission.

The off-center version is strongest because the path enters from the lower left and leads to the cyclist. I left extra space in front of the cyclist.

Feedback.

The chosen frame has a clear visual path and useful lead room. The horizon tilts slightly, so add an edge-and-horizon check before pressing the shutter.

L03 · Direction and quality of lightdemonstrated

What was learned.

Front light makes exposure predictable but can flatten texture. Side light reveals form because one side is brighter than the other. Back light can separate a subject from the background, but bright areas may clip. Soft light creates gradual transitions; hard light creates sharp shadows. Judge light by direction, contrast, and what detail matters most.

Practice.

Photograph one object with front, side, and back light. Compare texture, shadow, and highlight detail.

Learner submission.

Side light showed the mug texture best. Back light made a bright rim, but the window became pure white.

Feedback.

The comparison correctly identifies the effect of direction. The clipped window is acceptable only if it is not important; next time lower exposure or change framing and check the brightest area.

L04 · Focus and depth of fielddeveloping

What was learned.

Focus tells the viewer what must be sharp. A wider aperture such as f/2.8 often gives shallower depth of field than f/8, but camera-to-subject distance and focal length also matter. Moving closer usually makes the focused zone feel thinner. For a portrait, focus on the nearest eye; for a layered scene, decide whether separation or front-to-back detail serves the story.

Practice.

Photograph the same foreground object at f/2.8 and f/8 without changing position. Predict and then compare the background.

Learner submission.

I predicted f/8 would make the background blurrier. The actual f/8 frame showed more background detail.

Feedback.

The observation corrects the prediction: f/8 generally increases depth of field, while f/2.8 gives stronger separation. Keep the corrected relationship in the review list.

L05 · Freezing and showing motiondemonstrated

What was learned.

Fast shutter speeds freeze motion; slow speeds allow blur. Neither is automatically better. Freeze a gesture when detail matters, or use blur when movement itself is the subject. When shutter speed becomes faster, less light reaches the sensor, so aperture or ISO may need to change.

Practice.

Create one frozen-motion frame and one intentional-motion-blur frame. State the shutter speed and compensation used.

Learner submission.

I used 1/500 s at ISO 1600 to freeze a bicycle, then 1/20 s at ISO 200 while panning for blur.

Feedback.

Both choices match the intent, and the ISO changes show exposure compensation. The panning frame still has an identifiable rider, which makes the blur purposeful.

L06 · White balance and colourdemonstrated

What was learned.

White balance tells the camera what should appear neutral. Auto white balance is convenient, but mixed window and indoor light can create inconsistent colour. A fixed preset or manual Kelvin value can keep a sequence consistent. Colour can also carry meaning: warm tones may feel intimate, while cool tones can feel distant, but context matters.

Practice.

Photograph the same scene under mixed light with auto and a fixed setting. Choose the more coherent result and explain.

Learner submission.

Auto shifted between frames. The fixed 4300 K set stayed consistent, although the window side looked slightly blue.

Feedback.

The choice prioritizes consistency and acknowledges the remaining mixed-light limitation. That is a sound trade-off; repositioning the subject could reduce the two-colour split.

L07 · Five-photo storydemonstrated

What was learned.

A photo story needs progression, not seven unrelated strong frames. Establish the place, introduce the subject, show a meaningful detail, include an action, and close with a change or resolution. Visual consistency helps the sequence feel intentional; repetition and variation create rhythm.

Practice.

Create and order five photographs about a morning bicycle commute. Add one sentence explaining each frame’s role.

Learner submission.

1 establishes the quiet street; 2 introduces the rider; 3 shows hands fastening the helmet; 4 uses panning blur during departure; 5 ends with the empty bike rack at work.

Feedback.

The sequence has a clear arc and uses a detail and motion frame for variety. Frame 1 is slightly underexposed, but the overall set is coherent. The final work demonstrates intentional visual choices rather than accidental settings.
