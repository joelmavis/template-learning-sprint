Practice & Feedback

L01 · Introducing yourself

What was learned.

A short introduction can follow three moves: greeting, identity, and one useful detail. Use “I am” or “I’m” before a name or adjective, and use “I’m from …” for origin. A question such as “What about you?” returns the turn.

Practice.

Write a three-sentence introduction and ask one question.

Learner submission.

Hello, I’m Mina. I from Seoul and I am here for a design workshop. What about you?

Feedback.

The message is clear. Add the missing verb: “I’m from Seoul.” The final question keeps the conversation moving.

L02 · Checking understanding

What was learned.

Communication repair is a normal skill. Use a brief signal, a specific request, and confirmation: “Sorry, could you say that again?” “Could you speak more slowly?” “Do you mean Tuesday?” These phrases are more useful than silently guessing.

Practice.

Rewrite: “What? Talk more slow.” Then add a confirmation question about a meeting time.

Learner submission.

Sorry, can you say it more slow? Do you mean meeting at three?

Feedback.

Polite intent is good. Use the adverb “more slowly,” and include the article or full clause: “Do you mean the meeting is at three?”

L03 · Talking about where you are from

What was learned.

Origin and current residence may be different. “Where are you from?” can be answered with a city or country. “I was born in …, but I live in … now” gives both. Follow with “What’s it like?” to invite description.

Practice.

Write two sentences about birthplace and current city, then ask what the other city is like.

Learner submission.

I born in Busan, but I live in Seoul now. What it is like?

Feedback.

Use the passive form “I was born in Busan” and question order “What is it like?” The contrast with “but” is correct.

L04 · Describing a daily routine

What was learned.

Daily routines usually use present simple. With he, she, or it, add -s or -es to the verb. Time phrases often use “at” for a clock time and “in” for a part of day. Sequence words—first, then, after that, finally—make a routine easy to follow.

Practice.

Describe another person’s morning in four linked sentences.

Learner submission.

First, she wake up at seven. Then she has breakfast. After that she go to work at eight. Finally, she check email.

Feedback.

The sequence is easy to follow. Third-person verbs need -s: wakes, goes, and checks. “Has” is already correct.

L05 · Making suggestions

What was learned.

Two useful patterns are “Let’s + base verb” and “How about + verb-ing?” Modal “could” also takes a base verb: “We could meet earlier.” A response can accept—“That sounds good”—or adjust—“I can’t at six. How about seven?”

Practice.

Correct “We can to go for coffee” and write one alternative suggestion.

Learner submission.

We can go for coffee. How about meet at six?

Feedback.

“We can go” is correct because a modal takes the base verb. After “How about,” use the -ing form: “How about meeting at six?”

L06 · Talking about other people

What was learned.

A clear description can name the relationship, role, and one supported quality: “I work with Ana. She is a designer. She is good at explaining complex ideas.” Avoid broad praise without an example. “Good at” is followed by a noun or verb-ing form.

Practice.

Describe a colleague in three sentences and name one concrete strength.

Learner submission.

I work with Lena. She is a product designer. She is good in design systems and explains decisions clearly.

Feedback.

The concrete example is useful. The fixed phrase is “good at,” so write “She is good at design systems.” The final clause supports the description.
